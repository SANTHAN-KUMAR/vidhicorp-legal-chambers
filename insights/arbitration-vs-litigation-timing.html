Arbitration vs Litigation in India: When Arbitration Is Actually Faster, and When It Isn't

Arbitration is sold as the fast alternative to court. It often is. It is not automatically — and knowing the difference before a dispute clause is drafted matters more than knowing it during one.

Dispute Resolution July 2026 · 9 min read

By Jankee Ballabh Pandey, Founder & Managing Partner, VidhiCorp Legal Chambers

Key takeaways: Arbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 is built for speed — a 12-month tribunal deadline under Section 29A — but that speed depends on the dispute. It's strongest for technical, confidentiality-sensitive, or cross-border matters between cooperating parties; it weakens for multi-party disputes, urgent interim relief, and small claims where ad hoc arbitrator fees can rival litigation costs. The real decision gets made when the dispute resolution clause is drafted, not when the dispute arrives.

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, as amended, builds speed into the process by design: Section 29A requires an arbitral tribunal to make its award within twelve months of completion of pleadings, extendable by six months by consent of the parties, and only beyond that with the court's permission. On paper, that is a firm, predictable timeline that most Indian civil litigation cannot match, where a commercial suit can easily run several years through trial alone. In practice, whether arbitration actually delivers on that promise depends heavily on the kind of dispute in front of it — and conflating "arbitration" with "automatically faster" is one of the more expensive assumptions a business can build into a contract.

Where arbitration reliably wins on time and outcome

  • Technical or specialist disputes, where the parties can appoint an arbitrator with actual subject-matter expertise — construction, engineering, complex commercial accounting, IT services — rather than relying on a generalist court to build that expertise mid-trial through expert witnesses and lengthy cross-examination.
  • Confidentiality-sensitive matters, where the parties do not want the dispute, or the commercial terms underlying it, on the public record the way a court judgment typically would be.
  • Cross-border contracts, where an arbitral award is generally easier to enforce internationally under the New York Convention (to which India is a signatory) than a foreign court judgment would be, since enforcement of foreign judgments in India is more limited and jurisdiction-dependent.
  • Disputes between two parties who both intend to honor the process — arbitration's speed advantage depends heavily on both sides engaging constructively with the timetable; it erodes quickly when one side uses procedural tactics (repeated adjournment requests, jurisdictional challenges, discovery disputes) to stall what is meant to be a streamlined process.

Where the speed advantage narrows, or disappears

  • Multi-party disputes where not every party is bound by the same arbitration agreement. Arbitration generally cannot bind a party who never agreed to it, which can force parallel proceedings — arbitration for some parties, litigation for others — eliminating much of the efficiency gain and risking inconsistent outcomes across the two forums on overlapping facts.
  • Urgent interim relief. Section 9 of the Act allows courts to grant interim measures even where arbitration is the agreed mechanism, and in practice, genuinely urgent relief — an injunction, an asset freeze — is still routinely sought through the courts rather than waiting for a tribunal to be constituted, which itself can take weeks.
  • Post-award challenges. An award is not necessarily the end of the matter. A challenge under Section 34, and a further appeal under Section 37, can add years to final resolution — meaning the twelve-month tribunal timeline is not the full picture of how long an arbitrated dispute actually takes to fully and finally close, once a losing party is determined to contest the outcome.
  • Cost, for smaller claims. Ad hoc arbitrator fees in India are not standardized the way court fees are, and for a comparatively small claim, three-member-tribunal arbitration costs can approach or exceed what litigation would have cost, eroding the economic case for choosing arbitration in the first place. Institutional arbitration (through bodies with published fee schedules) mitigates this somewhat but adds its own administrative cost layer.

Arbitration vs litigation: a practical comparison for Indian commercial contracts

Litigation in Indian civil courts offers broader discovery and document production, a right of appeal built into the ordinary court hierarchy, no need to separately fund a tribunal, and — for disputes involving fraud allegations — a jurisdiction Indian courts have historically been more comfortable exercising, though the Supreme Court has narrowed the "fraud exception" to arbitrability considerably in recent years, meaning fraud allegations no longer automatically defeat an arbitration clause. Arbitration offers a private forum, party control over the choice of decision-maker, a statutory outer time limit the tribunal stage must respect, and materially better prospects of cross-border enforcement. Neither is categorically "better" — the right choice depends on the dispute a specific contract is actually likely to produce, which is a question worth answering at drafting stage, not after a disagreement has already started.

The decision that actually matters is made at drafting stage

By the time a dispute exists, the choice between arbitration and litigation has usually already been made — in the dispute resolution clause signed months or years earlier, often copied from a template without much thought. The more useful question isn't "which is faster in general," but "for the kind of dispute this specific contract is likely to produce — payment default, IP infringement, a multi-party construction dispute, a data breach — which mechanism actually resolves it fastest and most enforceably." That is a clause worth negotiating deliberately, not adopting as unread boilerplate.

Frequently asked questions

Does arbitration in India always have to finish within twelve months?

The arbitral tribunal is required under Section 29A of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 to make its award within twelve months of completion of pleadings, extendable by six months by party consent, and beyond that only with court approval. This covers the tribunal stage only — it does not include the time taken for any subsequent challenge to the award, which is where much of arbitration's real-world delay in India actually accumulates.

Can I get urgent interim relief while an arbitration is pending in India?

Yes. Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act allows a party to approach a court for interim measures — an injunction, an asset freeze, preservation of property — before, during, or after arbitral proceedings but before enforcement of the award. This exists precisely because a tribunal may not be constituted quickly enough to address genuine urgency, so parties routinely go to court for interim relief even where the substantive dispute is bound for arbitration.

What happens if only some parties to a dispute are bound by an arbitration agreement?

Arbitration generally cannot bind a party who never agreed to it. In multi-party disputes — a construction project with a main contractor, subcontractors and a client, for instance — where not everyone signed the same arbitration agreement, this can force parallel proceedings: arbitration for the parties who agreed to it, litigation for the rest, which tends to eliminate much of arbitration's efficiency advantage and can produce inconsistent findings across the two forums.

Is an arbitral award in India final, or can it still be appealed?

An award is not automatically final. It can be challenged under Section 34 of the Act on limited statutory grounds (not on the merits generally), and a further appeal lies under Section 37 against certain orders. A Section 34 challenge, and any appeal from it, can add substantial time to final resolution — meaning the twelve-month tribunal timeline is often only the first stage of how long a dispute actually takes to fully and finally close.
