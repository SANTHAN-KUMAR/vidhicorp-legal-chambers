Corporate Compliance Calendar for India: Building One That Survives a Growing Company

A compliance calendar built for a ten-person company and never rebuilt is one of the most common ways a growing business ends up in a regulator's inbox.

Regulatory August 2026 · 9 min read

By Jankee Ballabh Pandey, Founder & Managing Partner, VidhiCorp Legal Chambers

Key takeaways: Split your compliance calendar into deadline-based obligations (fixed dates: ROC filings, GST returns, TDS, PF/ESI, POSH annual report) and event-based obligations (triggered by what the business does: new office, new director, new headcount threshold). Assign named ownership per line, review quarterly, and keep a state-wise register the moment you operate in more than one state.

Most companies start with a compliance calendar that works: one spreadsheet, one owner, a handful of recurring dates — ROC annual filings, GST returns, TDS deposits, PF and ESI contributions. It works because the business is small enough that one person can hold the whole picture in their head. The failure mode isn't a bad calendar. It's a good calendar that was never rebuilt as the business outgrew it — and for a growing Indian company, that outgrowing tends to happen faster than the compliance function keeps pace with.

Two kinds of compliance, and why the distinction matters

Compliance obligations for an Indian company broadly fall into two categories, and a calendar that treats them the same way will eventually miss one of them.

  • Deadline-based compliance recurs on a fixed schedule regardless of what the business does day to day: annual filings under the Companies Act (AOC-4, MGT-7/MGT-7A), periodic GST returns, TDS deposits and quarterly TDS returns, monthly PF and ESI contributions, and the POSH Act's annual report to the district officer. These are predictable and can be calendared well in advance — the failure here is usually administrative (a payment missed, a filing forgotten), not conceptual.
  • Event-based compliance is triggered by something the business actually does: a board resolution for share allotment or a change of directors, a new state registration when the business opens an office in a new location, a fresh shops and establishments registration, an amendment to employment policies once headcount crosses a statutory threshold (POSH's ten-employee threshold for an Internal Committee is a classic example), or a new regulatory licence once the company enters a regulated activity. These don't show up on a fixed calendar at all — they show up only when someone remembers to ask the question at the moment the triggering event happens.

Where a single-owner calendar breaks down as a company scales

As headcount, entities, or office locations multiply, three things tend to happen at once: the person who built the original calendar is no longer the person best placed to know about every new trigger event happening across a now-larger organisation; obligations start varying by state (shops and establishments rules, for instance, are state legislation, not central law, and differ in form and renewal cycle accordingly); and the sheer volume of dates outgrows what a single reminder sitting in one person's inbox can reliably catch.

A specific pattern worth naming: the compliance gap usually isn't visible until it's tested — a labour inspection, a due diligence exercise ahead of a funding round, or an incident that triggers a POSH complaint against a company whose Internal Committee quietly lapsed eighteen months earlier because no one reconstituted it. The calendar looked fine on paper the whole time; it just wasn't actually being executed against.

A framework that scales with the business

  1. Separate the two categories explicitly. Maintain a deadline-based register with hard dates, and a trigger-based checklist tied to specific business events (new hire above a policy threshold, new office, new director, new state of operation, new product line, new funding round).
  2. Assign ownership, not just reminders. Each compliance line needs a named, accountable person — not a shared calendar invite that quietly becomes no one's responsibility once the original owner moves on or changes role.
  3. Review quarterly with legal or compliance counsel, specifically to catch trigger-based items the internal team wouldn't necessarily know to flag on its own — a new state of operation is a legal question before it becomes an HR or finance one, and by the time HR or finance notices, the registration deadline may already have passed.
  4. Keep a state-wise and entity-wise register the moment the business operates in more than one state or through more than one legal entity, rather than retrofitting one after a gap is discovered during, say, investor due diligence.
  5. Build in a change-management trigger. Every time the business adds a state, a product line, a headcount milestone, or a new regulated activity, that event itself should generate a compliance-calendar review — not wait for the next scheduled quarterly check.

Why this matters more at fundraising and diligence stage

A compliance calendar that looks adequate for day-to-day operations often gets tested hardest during investor due diligence or an acquisition process, when a buyer's counsel systematically checks every state registration, every POSH Internal Committee constitution, every ROC filing history against the company's actual operating footprint. Gaps that were invisible in ordinary business become negotiating leverage for the other side, or a closing condition that delays the transaction. Building the calendar properly before that point is materially cheaper than remediating it under deal pressure.

Frequently asked questions

What's the biggest compliance risk for a fast-growing company in India specifically?

Event-based obligations that never make it onto any calendar because no one thought to ask the compliance question when the triggering event happened — a new office opened without a corresponding state shops and establishments registration, a headcount threshold crossed without the resulting HR policy update, a new director appointed without the ROC filing that follows.

How often should a company compliance calendar actually be reviewed?

At minimum quarterly, and additionally at any point the business crosses a structural threshold — a new state of operation, a new legal entity, a material increase in headcount, or a new regulated business line. A calendar reviewed only once a year is, in practice, a calendar that misses the trigger-based items that matter most.

What are the key annual ROC compliance filings every private company in India needs to track?

For a typical private limited company, the recurring annual filings under the Companies Act, 2013 include the financial statements (Form AOC-4) and the annual return (Form MGT-7 or MGT-7A for small companies/OPCs), both due within statutory timelines of the AGM, along with any DIN KYC filings (DIR-3 KYC) for directors and, where applicable, cost audit or secretarial audit filings. These sit on the deadline-based half of the calendar described in this article — the event-based half is where growing companies typically fall behind.

Does a compliance calendar need to be different for a company operating in more than one Indian state?

Yes, materially. Several compliance obligations that feel uniform at head-office level are actually state legislation — shops and establishments registration and renewal being the clearest example — and vary in form, fee and renewal cycle from state to state. A company operating from Gurugram and expanding into, say, Bengaluru or Mumbai needs a state-wise register from the point of the first out-of-state office, not retrofitted after a gap is discovered.
