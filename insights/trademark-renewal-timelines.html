Trademark Renewal in India: Timelines, TM-R Filing and What Businesses Routinely Get Wrong

A registered trademark in India lasts ten years and can be renewed indefinitely — but the businesses that lose marks almost never lose them in a dispute. They lose them to a missed date.

Intellectual Property September 2026 · 9 min read

By Jankee Ballabh Pandey, Founder & Managing Partner, VidhiCorp Legal Chambers

Key takeaways: An Indian trademark registration is valid for ten years and renewable indefinitely, but renewal is not automatic. File Form TM-R up to a year before expiry; a missed date still allows a one-year restoration window with a surcharge; after that, the mark is liable for removal and a fresh application loses the original priority date. Multi-class portfolios need per-class tracking, not a single "trademark renewed" checkbox.

A registered trademark in India is valid for ten years from the date of application, and can be renewed for further ten-year periods indefinitely under the Trade Marks Act, 1999. In principle, this makes a trademark one of the most durable assets a business owns — unlike a patent, which expires for good after twenty years, a well-maintained trademark can outlive the company that first filed it. In practice, trademark renewal in India is also one of the easiest rights to lose without anyone doing anything wrong — simply by letting a date pass.

The trademark renewal window: how long you actually have

Under the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, an application for renewal — filed on Form TM-R — can be submitted up to one year before the mark's ten-year term expires. This is deliberately generous: the Registry wants renewals filed early, not at the last moment. If that window is missed and the registration lapses, the consequence is not immediate loss. Rule 58 gives the registered proprietor a further one-year restoration window after expiry, during which the mark can still be renewed on payment of a surcharge over the standard fee.

It is only after that combined window — up to a year before expiry, plus a year after — closes without action that the mark becomes formally liable for removal from the Register of Trade Marks. Once removed, the priority date that came with the original registration is gone. A fresh application starts the clock over, filed at the back of a queue that may, in the intervening period, now include a confusingly similar mark filed by someone else.

Where businesses actually go wrong on trademark renewal

  • Treating renewal as the trademark agent's problem, not the business's. A renewal reminder sent to an old contact, an inbox nobody checks any more, or an agent relationship that quietly lapsed when a retainer wasn't renewed, is a common cause of missed trademark renewal dates in India — and the consequence lands on the business that owns the mark, not the agent who once filed it.
  • Tracking the brand, not the class-wise registrations. A single brand is frequently registered across several classes under the Nice Classification (goods and services) — apparel in one class, retail services in another, software in a third. Each class-wise registration carries its own filing date and, therefore, its own renewal date if the classes were added at different times. A renewal calendar built around "the trademark" as a single line item, rather than each class-wise entry, quietly drops coverage on whichever classes were added later.
  • Stale ownership and correspondence records. Renewal notices and other Registry correspondence go to the address and email recorded against the registration. A change in registered office, an internal restructuring, or a change of trademark agent that was never reflected on the register means the notice may never reach anyone positioned to act on it — the Registry has no obligation to chase a proprietor who hasn't kept its own records current.
  • Assuming continued use protects the mark on its own. Continued commercial use of a mark does not substitute for renewal under Indian trademark law. An unrenewed, removed mark can be applied for afresh by a third party, and the original owner's prior use becomes, at best, a defensive argument in a subsequent dispute — not a right that was automatically preserved by trading under the name.
  • Losing track of Madrid Protocol designations separately from direct Indian filings. A business that holds both a directly-filed Indian trademark and an international registration designating India through the Madrid Protocol is really running two renewal systems in parallel — one through the Indian Trade Marks Registry, one centrally through WIPO. Treating them as a single renewal event is a frequent, avoidable error for exporters and multinational brand owners.

What the cost of getting it wrong actually looks like

Losing a registration is rarely just a filing-fee problem. A removed mark forces a fresh application, which resets the priority date — meaning a competitor's confusingly similar filing made during the gap can now stand ahead of the original brand owner in the queue. It can also strip away the enforcement tools that come with registration: statutory infringement remedies under the Trade Marks Act are materially stronger than the common-law passing-off action a business is left arguing once its registration has lapsed. For a brand with any accumulated goodwill, that gap — however briefly it exists — is a real commercial exposure, not a paperwork technicality.

Building a renewal calendar that actually holds

The reliable version of this is unglamorous: a single register covering every mark, every class, every jurisdiction the business operates in beyond India, and every renewal date — reviewed at fixed intervals, not left to a single reminder email sent once a year by an external agent. For businesses with a growing trademark portfolio — new product lines, new markets, rebrands, an acquisition that brought in someone else's marks — that register needs a named owner inside the business itself, not only the external trademark agent, so that a change in service provider never quietly creates a gap in coverage.

A practical minimum: review the register quarterly, confirm the Registry's records show the business's current address and an inbox that is actually monitored, and treat any newly acquired or newly filed mark as an addition to the same single register rather than a separate, one-off item to remember on its own.

Frequently asked questions

Does a trademark lapse automatically if I miss the renewal date?

Not immediately. Under Rule 58 of the Trade Marks Rules, 2017, there is a one-year restoration window after expiry, available on payment of a surcharge, before the mark becomes liable for removal from the register. After that window closes, restoration is generally no longer possible and a fresh application is required, starting the priority date over.

Do all classes under one brand renew on the same date in India?

Only if they were filed on the same date. A brand registered across several Nice Classification classes at different times, or with classes added later, typically carries a separate renewal date for each class-wise registration — a calendar built around "the trademark" as a single entity, rather than each class-wise entry, quietly drops coverage.

What is the trademark renewal fee in India?

The Trade Marks Rules prescribe separate fees for e-filing and physical filing of Form TM-R, with a further surcharge payable if the mark is renewed during the one-year restoration window after expiry rather than before it. Fees are charged per class, per registration — a portfolio spanning multiple classes multiplies accordingly, which is one more reason a consolidated, business-owned renewal register is worth maintaining rather than leaving each class to be tracked separately by memory.

Can I renew an Indian trademark from a foreign jurisdiction, or one filed through the Madrid Protocol?

An international registration designating India under the Madrid Protocol is renewed centrally through WIPO, on its own ten-year cycle, separate from the renewal mechanics of a mark filed directly with the Indian Trade Marks Registry. A business holding both direct Indian filings and Madrid Protocol designations needs to track two different renewal systems, not one — a common source of the exact gap this article describes.
