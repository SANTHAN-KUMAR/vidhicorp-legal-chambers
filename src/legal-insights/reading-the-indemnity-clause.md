<p style="margin-bottom:0"><strong>Key takeaways:</strong> An indemnity clause under Indian contract law answers one question — who pays, and how much, when something goes wrong. The five things worth checking before signing: the definition of "Losses," any cap or basket, the survival period, the notice-and-defense procedure, and whether the indemnity is the sole remedy or sits alongside others. The wording decides the deal far more than the mere presence of the clause does.</p>
</div>

<p>An indemnity clause is meant to answer one question: if something goes wrong, who pays for it, and how much? Under Section 124 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a contract of indemnity is a promise to make good the loss caused to one party by the conduct of the other, or of a third party. That statutory definition is simple. The clauses that implement it in a real commercial contract usually are not — and the gap between the two is where disputes over indemnity clauses in India actually live.</p>

<h2>What the other side is actually reading for in your indemnity clause</h2>
<p>A counterparty's counsel reading an indemnity clause is rarely testing whether it exists. They are testing five things: what counts as a "loss," whether that loss is capped, how long the indemnity survives after the deal closes, what has to happen procedurally before a claim can be made, and whether the indemnity is the only remedy available or sits alongside others such as a claim for breach of contract or negligence.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Scope of "Losses."</strong> A broadly defined term — covering direct, indirect and consequential losses, along with legal costs, interest, and third-party claims — shifts significant risk onto the indemnifying party. A narrower definition, limited to direct losses reasonably foreseeable at the time of contracting, is a meaningfully different commercial deal, even though both versions read, on the surface, as "an indemnity clause."</li>
<li><strong>Caps and baskets.</strong> Is there a maximum indemnifiable amount, often expressed as a percentage of contract value or a fixed sum? Is there a minimum threshold — a "basket" — below which claims can't be brought at all, and if so, is it a deductible (only the excess above the threshold is recoverable) or a trigger (once crossed, the whole claim is recoverable)? These mechanics decide who absorbs small, routine issues versus material ones, and are frequently negotiated harder than the headline indemnity obligation itself.</li>
<li><strong>Survival period.</strong> An indemnity that survives indefinitely creates open-ended exposure for the indemnifying party; one that expires with the general contract term may leave the indemnified party unprotected against a loss that only surfaces later — a tax reassessment, a latent product defect, or an IP infringement claim discovered years after delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Notice and defense procedure.</strong> Many indemnity clauses require notice of a claim within a defined period, and give the indemnifying party the right to control (or at least participate in) the defense of a third-party claim. Missing a contractual notice deadline, even on an otherwise legitimate claim, can be fatal to recovering under the clause — a point that catches out indemnified parties who assume a valid loss is enough on its own.</li>
<li><strong>Sole remedy or cumulative.</strong> Some contracts state the indemnity is the exclusive remedy for the covered losses; others allow it alongside a separate claim for breach of contract or in tort. This materially affects negotiating leverage if a dispute actually arises, since it determines whether a party is confined to the indemnity's specific caps and procedures or can pursue a broader claim outside them.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Indemnity clauses in Indian M&A and commercial contracts specifically</h2>
<p>In share purchase and business transfer agreements governed by Indian law, indemnity clauses typically sit alongside — and are carefully cross-referenced against — representations and warranties, disclosure schedules, and a separate limitation of liability section. A well-drafted Indian SPA usually distinguishes "fundamental" warranties (title, capacity, authority) which may carry uncapped or lightly capped indemnities and a longer survival period, from "business" warranties (compliance, financial statements, material contracts) which typically carry a standard cap (often a percentage of purchase price) and a survival period tied to the statutory limitation period or a negotiated shorter window. Getting this tiering wrong — applying one blanket cap and one blanket survival period to every warranty — is a common drafting shortcut that under-protects the buyer on the matters that matter most, or over-exposes the seller on routine ones.</p>

<h2>Reading it before signing, not after a claim arrives</h2>
<p>The practical discipline is to read every indemnity clause as if the loss has already happened: which party is holding the risk in that scenario, and does the cap, the basket and the survival period actually reflect what was negotiated on price and risk allocation elsewhere in the deal — or did the indemnity clause quietly move that allocation on its own, buried in language that reads as boilerplate but isn't?</p>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Is an indemnity clause the same as a limitation of liability clause?</h3>
<p>No. An indemnity clause creates an obligation to compensate for a defined category of loss; a limitation of liability clause caps or excludes liability generally, often across the whole contract. The two frequently interact in the same agreement — an indemnity can sit inside, alongside, or explicitly outside a liability cap — and need to be read together, not in isolation, to know what a party is actually exposed to.</p>
<h3>Can an indemnity clause be enforced if the loss wasn't foreseeable?</h3>
<p>It depends entirely on the drafting. A narrowly worded clause tied to specific, defined trigger events will not extend to unrelated losses; a broadly worded one covering "any and all losses arising out of or in connection with" the agreement can extend much further, including to losses neither party turned their mind to at signing. This is precisely why the wording, not merely the existence, of the clause is what matters in a dispute.</p>
<h3>What is the difference between a direct claim and a third-party claim under an indemnity?</h3>
<p>A direct claim is one party claiming against the other for its own loss (say, a breach of warranty causing lost value). A third-party claim arises when an outside party sues the indemnified party, who then looks to the indemnifying party to cover the resulting loss and, often, to control the defense. Indemnity clauses commonly set out separate notice and procedural requirements for each — missing the distinction, or the notice deadline attached to it, can be fatal to recovering under an otherwise valid clause.</p>
<h3>Under Indian law, is a contract of indemnity limited to loss caused by the other contracting party?</h3>
<p>Section 124 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 defines a contract of indemnity as a promise to save the promisee from loss caused by the conduct of the promisor, or by the conduct of any other person. Commercial indemnity clauses in India are drafted more broadly than this statutory baseline in most cases — extending to third-party claims, regulatory penalties, and specified categories of loss the parties choose to allocate — which is exactly why the negotiated wording carries more practical weight than the statutory default.</p>
