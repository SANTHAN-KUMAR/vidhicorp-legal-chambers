<div class="article-cta" style="margin-top:0;margin-bottom:36px;text-align:left">
<p style="margin-bottom:0"><strong>Key takeaways:</strong> A POSH policy document is table stakes. Real compliance means a validly constituted Internal Committee (with the mandatory external member), inquiry timelines actually met (90 days to inquire, 10 days to report, 60 days for the employer to act), an annual report filed with the District Officer, IC reconstitution before terms lapse, and ongoing — not one-time — awareness training.</p>
</div>

<p>The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 requires every employer with ten or more employees to constitute an Internal Committee (IC) and adopt a policy. A decade after the Act came into force, most organisations of any size have the policy document — it's become a standard onboarding attachment. The gap that persists, and the one that actually gets tested when a complaint arrives, is almost always in what sits behind the document.</p>

<h2>Internal Committee composition that actually satisfies the Act</h2>
<p>An Internal Committee has specific, non-negotiable composition requirements under the POSH Act: a Presiding Officer who is a senior woman employee, at least two members from among employees committed to the cause of women or with relevant experience, and — critically, and the requirement organisations most often get wrong — one external member from a non-governmental organisation or association familiar with issues of sexual harassment. An IC missing that external member, or one that has quietly lapsed off the committee without replacement after resigning or leaving the organisation, is not validly constituted, regardless of how thorough the written policy sitting in the HR handbook is.</p>

<h2>The parts of POSH compliance that live outside the policy document</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Inquiry timelines.</strong> The IC is required to complete an inquiry within 90 days of a complaint, and submit its report within 10 days of completing the inquiry; the employer must then act on the recommendations within 60 days of receiving the report. A policy that recites these timelines correctly but sits inside an organisation with no operational process to actually meet them is exposed the first time a complaint genuinely arrives and the clock starts running.</li>
<li><strong>The annual report to the District Officer.</strong> Employers are required to file an annual report with the District Officer covering complaints received, disposed of and pending. This is a recurring compliance obligation, not a one-time setup task completed when the policy was first adopted — and it is distinct from any separate board-report disclosure certain companies must also make under the Companies Act or SEBI's listing regulations.</li>
<li><strong>Committee reconstitution.</strong> IC membership carries a defined term (not exceeding three years per member), and organisations frequently fail to reconstitute the committee when a member's term lapses, or when a member exits the organisation altogether — leaving an improperly constituted IC in place without anyone noticing until an inquiry is already underway and the composition itself becomes a point of challenge.</li>
<li><strong>Training that isn't a one-time onboarding module.</strong> Awareness training is expected on an ongoing basis, not delivered once to new joiners in an onboarding deck and never repeated for the existing workforce. This includes training for IC members specifically on inquiry procedure — a different, more detailed training need than general workplace awareness training for employees generally.</li>
<li><strong>Interim relief provisions.</strong> The Act allows the complainant to request interim measures during an inquiry — transfer of either party, granting leave, or other relief to prevent continued contact — a provision that exists and is legally available but is frequently unused simply because HR teams aren't operationally prepared to invoke it when a complaint is actually filed.</li>
<li><strong>Confidentiality obligations.</strong> Section 16 of the Act restricts disclosure of the identity of the complainant, respondent, witnesses and inquiry details, with specific consequences for breach. This applies to the organisation's own internal communications about a live complaint, not just external disclosure — an easy point to overlook when an inquiry becomes the subject of office speculation.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why the external member matters more than organisations expect</h2>
<p>Beyond the bare compliance requirement itself, the external member brings genuine independence to a process that would otherwise sit entirely within the reporting lines of the organisation — which matters both for the credibility of any finding the IC reaches, and for complainants' willingness to come forward at all, particularly where the respondent is senior to, or has influence over, the internal IC members. This is precisely the role many organisations choose to fill through an external POSH consultant or law firm engaged on an ongoing retainer, rather than searching for a suitable NGO representative only after a complaint has already been filed.</p>

<h2>A practical POSH compliance checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Confirm the IC is currently, validly constituted — Presiding Officer, at least two internal members, and the external member — with no lapsed terms.</li>
<li>Confirm the policy document reflects the organisation's actual reporting channel and current IC composition, not a stale version from an earlier year.</li>
<li>Confirm the last annual report was actually filed with the District Officer, and calendar the next one.</li>
<li>Confirm awareness training has been delivered in the last twelve months, not only at onboarding for employees who joined years ago.</li>
<li>Confirm IC members themselves have received training on inquiry procedure, timelines, and confidentiality obligations — not just general POSH awareness.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
<h3>Does a small company need an Internal Committee under the POSH Act?</h3>
<p>Any organisation with ten or more employees must constitute an Internal Committee (IC). Organisations below that threshold — and complaints against an employer itself, regardless of size — are addressed instead through a Local Committee constituted by the District Officer under the Act.</p>
<h3>What happens if our Internal Committee isn't properly constituted when a complaint arrives?</h3>
<p>An improperly constituted IC — missing the mandatory external member, or operating past a lapsed term without reconstitution — undermines the legal validity of any inquiry it conducts. This can expose the organisation to the complaint being escalated to the Local Committee, to procedural challenge of the inquiry's findings, and to regulatory scrutiny of the organisation's overall POSH compliance.</p>
<h3>Who qualifies as the mandatory external member of a POSH Internal Committee?</h3>
<p>The external member must be from a non-governmental organisation or association committed to the cause of women, or someone familiar with issues relating to sexual harassment — the Act's intent is to bring independence and subject-matter familiarity into a process that would otherwise sit entirely within the organisation's own reporting lines. This is precisely the role many organisations engage external POSH consultants or legal practices to fill on an ongoing basis.</p>
<h3>Is the POSH annual report the same as the Companies Act board report disclosure?</h3>
<p>No, they are two separate obligations that are often confused. The POSH Act requires the employer to file an annual report with the District Officer covering the number of complaints received, disposed of, and pending, among other details. Separately, certain companies under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI's listing regulations must additionally disclose POSH-related information in the board's report or business responsibility report. An organisation can satisfy one and still miss the other.</p>
<h3>How often should POSH awareness training actually be conducted?</h3>
<p>The Act contemplates ongoing awareness efforts, not a single onboarding session delivered once to new joiners and never repeated. Good practice — and increasingly, what regulators and diligence processes expect to see evidenced — is at least annual refresher training for the existing workforce, alongside onboarding training for new employees and specific training for IC members on inquiry procedure.</p>
