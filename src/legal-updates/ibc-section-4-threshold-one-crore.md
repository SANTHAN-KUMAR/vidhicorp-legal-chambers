## What changed

By notification under the proviso to Section 4 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, the Central Government specified **₹1 crore** as the minimum amount of default for initiating a corporate insolvency resolution process, in place of the earlier ₹1 lakh.

The threshold has held since. It applies to applications under Sections 7, 9 and 10 alike.

## Why it matters to a business

For several years after the Code came into force, a Section 9 demand notice was the fastest commercial-recovery instrument available to an operational creditor. A modest unpaid invoice could put a solvent company in front of the NCLT, and settlements followed quickly.

That lever is now unavailable to most trade creditors. An operational creditor owed ₹40 lakh has no route under the Code and must recover through the ordinary civil or arbitral process — which is slower, and which is precisely where the Commercial Courts Act mediation step now bites.

## What to do

- Do not build a recovery strategy around the threat of insolvency unless the default genuinely crosses ₹1 crore. A Section 8 notice issued below the threshold achieves nothing and signals inexperience.
- Where several invoices are outstanding against the same corporate debtor, check whether the aggregate default crosses the threshold and whether the claims are properly aggregable.
- For sub-threshold exposure, the practical answers are contractual: securing dues, interest clauses that are actually enforceable, personal or corporate guarantees, and a summary-suit route where the documents support it.
- Review your credit policy against the threshold. Exposure per customer is now a legal question as well as a finance one.
