## The rule

Section 29A of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 requires the arbitral award in a matter other than an international commercial arbitration to be made within **twelve months from the date of completion of pleadings** under Section 23(4). The parties may, by consent, extend that period by a further **six months**. Beyond eighteen months, the mandate of the tribunal **terminates** unless the period is extended by the court, which may do so only for sufficient cause and may impose conditions, including reduction of fees where the delay is attributable to the tribunal.

The clock runs from completion of pleadings, not from the tribunal's constitution — a point that is still misread in practice.

## Why it matters

Parties conduct arbitrations as though the timeline is aspirational. It is not. When a mandate terminates, the proceeding stops where it stands, and reviving it requires a court application that the other side is entitled to resist. The party that suffers most is usually the claimant with the stronger case, which has already spent the fees and led the evidence.

Two recurring failures:

- **Consent extensions taken too late.** The six-month extension is by agreement of the parties. Once relations have broken down — which is the normal condition by month eleven — the other side has no reason to agree.
- **No record of when pleadings closed.** If the tribunal has not recorded the date of completion of pleadings in a procedural order, the parties end up litigating the start date of their own deadline.

## What to do

- Ask the tribunal to record the **date of completion of pleadings** in a procedural order, expressly, at the time it occurs.
- Diarise the twelve-month and eighteen-month dates at the outset and review the procedural timetable against them, not against the hearing calendar.
- Where the schedule is slipping, seek the consent extension **early**, while the counterparty still has an incentive to cooperate.
- If a court extension is needed, file before expiry where possible, and be ready to explain the delay with specifics.
- At the drafting stage, a well-constructed arbitration clause — seat, number of arbitrators, appointing authority, language — removes months of preliminary skirmishing that eat into the same twelve months.
